## 0. Logline
You were left with a question. No. 04 ended: "So what will you be eating in twenty one hundred?" Over seven mornings,
from a Flemish village in 1500 to a São Paulo kitchen today and on to 2100, you eat on fast days, meet the tomato before
anyone knew what to do with it, bottle a summer, watch a navy ship fall sick on white rice, wait for rain in Punjab, and
open a delivery app. Then you find out what feeds roughly half of everyone alive: **fertiliser made from the air.**
And 2100 answers No. 04's question from published evidence only.

**fd03 · promise (3.1)**
Seven mornings, from fifteen hundred to twenty one hundred. In each one, you wake up somewhere new, and you sit down to eat. You start with whatever the harvest gave you. You end with a plate that hasn't been cooked yet. And at the end, you'll find out what feeds half the world.

**fd04 · 1500**
You wake up in fifteen hundred, in a village in Flanders. It's still dark. The bread oven's warm, and the kitchen smells of rye and smoke.
Did you know that here, on up to four days in ten, eating meat is against the rules? [beat] Fridays. Lent. Advent. The Church calls them fast days.
Your day runs on bread. You're in the fields from first light. And most of what your family spends goes on food. Historians put it at seventy to eighty percent.
So what is food to you? [beat] The harvest. Whatever the sky decides to give you.
Remember those fast days. They come back at the end.
Because across the ocean, Spanish ships are finding plants no one here has ever tasted. And one of them will end up in a kitchen in Naples, where nobody's quite sure what to do with it.

**fd05 · 1692**
You wake up in sixteen ninety two, in Naples. The streets are loud, and someone's roasting something red over the coals.
Did you know that tomatoes had been in Italy for almost a hundred and fifty years before anyone printed a recipe for tomato sauce? [beat] It's being printed here. This year.
When the fruit first arrived, an Italian doctor described it in his chapter on mandrake, a famously poisonous plant. In some places, tomatoes were grown to be looked at, not eaten.
The man printing the recipe is Antonio Latini. He calls it sauce in the Spanish style. Roasted tomatoes, onion, chilli and thyme. And it isn't for pasta. It's for boiled meat. Pasta with tomato sauce takes about another hundred years.
So what is food now? [beat] A world of new crops. Plants from the other side of the ocean, on your plate.
But in a Naples summer, food goes bad in days. Until a man near Paris learns to bottle a summer.

**fd06 · 1810**
You wake up in eighteen ten, in Massy, a village just south of Paris. Steam fills a workshop, and rows of sealed glass bottles sit in boiling water.
Did you know that the man who invented canned food had no idea why it worked? [beat] His name is Nicolas Appert. He used to make sweets.
Until now, keeping food means salting it, smoking it, drying it or pickling it.
Appert seals food in bottles and boils them. Somehow, it stays good. This year, the French government pays him twelve thousand francs. On one condition. He has to publish exactly how he does it.
So what is food now? [beat] Something you can keep. A summer, sealed in a bottle.
Soon the bottles become tin cans. And the can opener? That comes decades later. Until then, people were told to use a hammer and a chisel.
It takes about fifty years before Louis Pasteur shows what spoils food. Germs. But what's inside food is a bigger mystery. And on a Japanese navy ship, it's killing people.

**fd07 · 1884**
You wake up in eighteen eighty four, at Shinagawa, on Tokyo Bay. The wind's freezing, and a training ship called the Tsukuba is getting ready to sail.
Did you know that on a voyage like this one, the year before, almost half the crew got sick from what they ate? [beat] On the ship Ryujo, a hundred and sixty nine of three hundred and seventy six men got a disease called beriberi. Twenty five died.
The sailors ate almost nothing but white rice. Rice was free. Everything else cost money.
A navy doctor, Takaki Kanehiro, has been measuring what the men eat. He thinks something's missing. So he changes the menu. More barley, meat, milk, bread and vegetables.
The Tsukuba sails the same route. Fourteen men get sick. None die. And the fourteen? They hadn't eaten the new food.
So what is food now? [beat] Something to measure. What's inside it matters, not just how much.
He's right about the cure, and wrong about the cause. Decades later, scientists name the missing thing a vitamin. But knowing what to eat doesn't help if there isn't enough of it. And in India, there isn't.

**fd08 · 1966**
You wake up in nineteen sixty six, in a village in Punjab. The air's dry and dusty, and everyone's watching the sky for rain.
Did you know that the year before, India's prime minister asked the whole country to skip one meal a week? [beat] Lal Bahadur Shastri. There was war, there was drought, and there wasn't enough grain.
So India tries something new. This year, it imports eighteen thousand tonnes of wheat seed from Mexico. Short, sturdy plants, bred by Norman Borlaug's team, and backed here by scientists like M. S. Swaminathan.
But these seeds are hungry. They need water, and they need fertiliser. Lots of it. Remember that word. Fertiliser.
Within a few years, India's wheat harvest jumps to seventeen million tonnes. Not long before, it was barely twelve.
So what is food now? [beat] Something we engineer. Bred by scientists. Fed by factories.
And around the world, food gets cheaper, and more plentiful, than ever.

**fd09 · Today**
You wake up today, in São Paulo. Motorbikes buzz past with food deliveries, and your phone's already showing you breakfast.
Did you know that Brazil's government tells people to eat at regular times, and, whenever they can, with other people? [beat] It's in the country's official food guide.
And its golden rule isn't about calories. Choose fresh food and home-made meals over ultra-processed food.
That idea, ultra-processed, was put on the map here, by researchers led by Carlos Monteiro. It means factory-made products, built from food extracts and additives. Soft drinks. Packaged snacks.
In Brazil, they're about a fifth of the calories people eat. In the United States, they're more than half.
So what is food to you? [beat] Something made for you. By someone you'll never meet.
So here's the question you came for. Eight billion of us. One invention helps feed roughly half. What is it?
