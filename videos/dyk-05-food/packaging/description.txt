Today there's more food than ever. And yet about one in five deaths around the world is linked to diet. So did food give us our longer lives, or is it taking some of those years back? In 1800, life expectancy at birth, worldwide, was about 29 years. What about a baby born today? Guess before the answer.

Wake up seven times (1500, 1747, 1884, 1915, 1966, today and 2100) and sit down to seven plates: bread, porridge and dried fish on a Flemish fast day, ship's biscuit on a British warship, white rice on a Japanese training ship, cornbread on a Mississippi prison farm, flatbread and lentils in Punjab before the new wheat, rice and beans and a delivery app in São Paulo. Each time you see what that food did to the body and how long a newborn could expect to live. Watch how people saw food, rung by rung: fuel, medicine, something to measure, something we fix, enough at last, and now too much.

Then the answer. Food gave us years, but not alone: the doctor Thomas McKeown argued better food was the main reason we live longer, many historians disagree, and one study found clean water alone was behind nearly half the drop in deaths in big American cities in the early 1900s.

And in 2100? The UN expects life expectancy worldwide to reach about 82, the IPCC says warming has already slowed the growth of farm productivity, and the EAT-Lancet Commission says a mostly plant diet could prevent around 15 million early deaths a year. We follow that published evidence to one scenario, clearly labelled as a prediction, not a fact.
