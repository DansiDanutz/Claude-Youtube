It's quarter to eight and somewhere near you a school bell is about to ring. Right now about 1.4 billion children are in school, more than ever before. Five hundred years ago, most children never saw a classroom. They worked. And the word "school" itself first meant something you'd never guess.

And in 2100? Tutoring works, and a first World Bank pilot of an AI tutor in Nigeria is a start. Will every child have a tutor, and will the classroom even exist? We follow the published evidence (Bloom, the World Bank, OECD, UNESCO) to one scenario, clearly labelled as a prediction, not a fact.

Wake up seven times (1500, 1647, 1763, 1810, 1948, today and 2100) and watch how people saw school, rung by rung: something for the few, a way to read God's word, the king's order, a machine for teaching the poor by the hundred, a right and a ladder, and now always on. A prince with his own tutor in Tudor London, a 1647 law that begins by blaming the devil, the Prussian law that banned school holidays, Joseph Lancaster's thousand-pupil schools and their cages, the high-school boom, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the 2020 closures. Then the answer: what the word school really meant.
