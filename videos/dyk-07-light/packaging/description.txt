Why is light pollution bad, and why can't we see stars anymore? This is the history of light from 1500 to 2100. In Britain, light now costs less than one three-thousandth of what it did in 1800. You'd think we'd pocket the savings. We didn't. Here's your guess: think of ten people anywhere on Earth. How many of them live under a night sky that our lights have brightened? Two? Five? More than eight? Guess before the answer.

Wake up six times (1500, 1807, 1859, 1882, 1993 and today) and see how people lit the night: a rushlight and a curfew bell in a market town in Flanders, the first gas lamps on Pall Mall in London, kerosene and the first commercial oil well in Titusville, Pennsylvania, Edison's Pearl Street Station lighting a few blocks of lower Manhattan, and the blue LED made in Tokushima, Japan. Each time you see what one hour of light cost in work. Watch how people saw light, rung by rung: a luxury, a service, a liquid, a network, a crystal, and now something too cheap to switch off.

Then the answer. Every time light got cheaper, we used more, until it filled the sky. In 1500, the scarce thing was light. Today, the scarce thing is the dark.

And in 2100? Some places already protect the dark: since 2013, France has told shops to switch off their window lights by one in the morning, and Tucson, Arizona has had outdoor-lighting rules since 1972 to keep the sky dark for its observatories. We follow that published evidence to one scenario, clearly labelled as a prediction, not a fact.
