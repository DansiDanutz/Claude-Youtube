In 1799, George Washington's doctors drained, by some estimates, around two fifths of his blood in a single day. They were trained, and they meant well. So how did anyone ever find out what really works? Here's your guess: in 1948, 55 tuberculosis patients got a new drug and 52 got bed rest alone. After six months, how many of the 55 had died? Guess before the answer.

Wake up seven times (1537, 1799, 1847, 1941, 1948, today and 2100) and meet seven patients: a soldier with a gunshot wound in a French army camp near Turin, George Washington at Mount Vernon, a new mother in Vienna's General Hospital, a police officer in Oxford who got the first penicillin, tuberculosis patients on a British ward, and a child in Merca, Somalia, the town of the last natural case of smallpox. Each time you see what the doctor believed, what the treatment did and how long a newborn could expect to live. Watch how people saw medicine, rung by rung: balance, authority, a pattern, a cure, proof, and now prevention.

Then the answer. Medicine didn't get better because doctors got smarter. It got better when someone asked a simple question: compared with what? Semmelweis counted mothers' deaths before and after handwashing. The 1948 streptomycin trial let chance decide who got the drug, then counted. And the idea was older: in 1747, James Lind compared six cures for scurvy.

And in 2100? The UN expects life expectancy worldwide to reach about 82. But drug-resistant bacteria directly caused about 1.1 million deaths in 2021, and a study in The Lancet forecasts about 1.9 million a year by 2050 without more action. We follow that published evidence to one scenario, clearly labelled as a prediction, not a fact.

CORRECTION (in the 2100 section): the narration says resistance played a part in "four point seven million more" deaths. The 4.7 million deaths associated with antibiotic resistance in 2021 include the 1.1 million it directly caused; they are not on top of them.
